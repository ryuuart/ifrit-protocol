A page of running text behaves nothing like a single word set large. A word is a shape; a page is a texture. The eye reads a word by its outline and a page by its overall grey, and the two ask opposite things of whatever paints them. A fill that flatters five capitals across a poster can vanish entirely across four hundred lines of eight-point body, because the same gradient that swept from one letter to the next now has a whole column to cross and moves a hundredth as fast per glyph. The reverse is just as common: a field of fine noise that reads as texture on a headline turns the same body copy into static, since its features are now smaller than the strokes they sit inside. Neither failure shows up in a specimen that only ever paints one word.
Printers knew this long before anyone wrote a shader. A forme of type was inked by a roller that had to leave the same film on a capital as on a comma, and every trade manual spends its patience on evenness rather than on brilliance. Too much ink and the counters fill, the page goes muddy and the descenders bleed into the line beneath; too little and the letters break up, the serifs drop out and the reader's eye starts to hunt. The compositor's answer was never a clever ink. It was a measured one, applied at a coverage the whole forme could carry, judged by holding the sheet at arm's length and squinting until the words dissolved and only the grey remained. That test still works, and nothing has replaced it.
Measure is the other half. A line long enough to hold eighteen words is a line the eye can lose its place in on the return sweep, and a line short enough to hold four is a line that breaks every phrase in the wrong place and leaves the right edge in tatters. Somewhere between sixty and seventy-five characters the two problems trade places, which is why so many books of very different centuries and very different faces arrive at nearly the same column. The number is not magic; it follows from how far a reader's eye can jump accurately and how much a single fixation takes in. Narrow the measure and the leading has to come down with it, or the column turns into a ladder of stripes.
Justification is where a page shows its manners. Setting both edges flush means the difference between the natural width of a line and the measure has to be spent somewhere, and the only places to spend it are the word gaps, the space between the letters, and the letters themselves. Spend it all on the gaps and a loose line opens holes that stack down the column into a river; spend it on the letters and the texture changes weight from line to line, which is worse, because the reader feels it without seeing why. A good setting spends a little in each place and asks the line breaker to find a set of breaks that keeps the whole paragraph even rather than making each line locally as full as it can be.
The grey a page settles at is a real quantity, not a metaphor. It is coverage: the fraction of the column's area that carries ink, averaged over an area large enough to include several lines. Type size, leading, measure, tracking, the weight of the face and the fit of its sidebearings all move it, and they can be traded against each other. A heavier face at a wider leading can reach the same grey as a lighter one set tight, and the two pages will read as equally dark from across a room while looking nothing alike up close. Anything laid over the text — a tint, a gradient, a field of noise, a photograph — is competing with that grey, and it usually wins.
Painting the glyphs themselves rather than the space behind them is the harder of the two. A background can be as busy as it likes so long as it stays far enough from the text in value; the letters keep their own contrast and the reader keeps their place. Paint inside the letterforms and every variation in the fill becomes a variation in the stroke, and a stroke a fraction of a millimetre wide has no room to carry a gradient at all. What the reader sees instead is a change of weight: pale letters look lighter, dark ones look bolder, and a passage that should be one voice starts to sound like three.
The mapping matters as much as the fill. If a material's unit square is laid over the whole block of text — from the cap top of the first line to the baseline of the last — then a ramp authored to cross the capitals of a wordmark now crosses four hundred lines instead, and the first paragraph gets the top three per cent of it. Every line is nearly one colour, and that colour drifts so slowly down the page that no reader would ever call it a gradient. Map the square to each line instead and the ramp repeats down the column like wallpaper. Map it to each glyph and the page turns into confetti. There is no default that is right for all three, which is exactly why a page has to be looked at.
Fields that move add a further problem, because a page of text is a great many small apertures onto the same moving thing. A ripple travelling across a headline is a legible motion: the word is wide, the wave is wide, and the eye follows one crest. The same ripple behind body copy is sampled by ten thousand disconnected strokes, and what the reader gets is not a wave but a shimmer — every letter changing value on its own schedule, none of them agreeing, the whole column boiling. The fix is never a faster shader. It is a slower wave, or a longer one, or an amplitude small enough that the change in value stays under what the eye notices while reading.
There is a threshold, and it is lower than most people expect. A reader will tolerate a good deal of colour variation in display type and almost none in a passage they are actually reading, because reading is a motor task as much as a visual one and anything that changes between fixations costs a re-fixation. Ten per cent of value swing across a paragraph is decoration. Thirty is a distraction. Beyond that the text stops being read and starts being looked at, which may well be the intention, but it should be the intention rather than an accident of a fill that was never tested at this size.
So a sheet like this one exists to be squinted at. Each panel takes the same passage, sets it in the same face at the same size in the same measure, and hands it to one preset paint. Nothing else differs. What the panels show is not whether a shader compiles — a test settles that in a millisecond and does — but what each fill does to a page: which ones hold their grey, which ones stripe, which ones dissolve into shimmer, and which ones are so dark in their lower half that the last lines of the column disappear into the paper.
Some of the answers are predictable and worth having written down anyway. A ramp in unit space is nearly flat here by construction, since the square it is mapped through is now the height of the whole block. A field with fine features will alias, because the strokes it is drawn inside are a pixel or two wide and the field has no idea. A field that is mostly transparent needs something under it, or the text simply is not there. A field with a strong dark region will eat whichever paragraph lands in it, and which paragraph that is depends on how long the passage is — which means the same paint on a different page is a different paint.
That last point is the one that argues for a page rather than a word. A wordmark's fill is a fixed composition: the same five letters, the same proportions, the same result every time it is drawn. A page's fill is a function of the text. Add a sentence and every line after it moves; the block grows taller, the unit square stretches, and the band of the gradient that was crossing the third paragraph is now crossing the fourth. Nothing in the code changed. A specimen that never sets more than a word cannot catch that, and a plate that sets a long passage catches it the first time anyone looks.
Paper is a participant, not a backdrop. A dark ground under light letters thickens them optically: the pale strokes bleed outward into the surrounding dark, and the same face reads half a weight heavier than it does black on white. Printers compensated by choosing a lighter cut for reversed setting, and screens have the same problem for the same reason. It matters here because every panel stands on one ground while the fills that cross the letters range from near-white to near-black, so a single passage can look under-set at the top of a panel and over-inked at the bottom without a single glyph having changed. Judging the ink means judging it against the paper it was pulled on, which is why the ground is stated once for the whole sheet and never varied per panel.
Leading is the quietest of the controls and the one a fill notices most. The space between the lines is where the ground shows through, and a column set tight offers the paint fewer and thinner windows onto the page beneath it than the same column set open. Open the leading and the block grows taller, which stretches whatever square the fill is mapped through and slows every gradient in it; close it and the block shortens, the gradient quickens, and bands that were spread over a page start landing inside single paragraphs. So the leading is not a neutral setting to be adjusted after the ink has been chosen. It is part of the composition, and a sheet that varies it while varying the paint has learned nothing about either.
What a good result looks like is easy to state and hard to reach: from a step back the panel should read as an even grey rectangle with no bands, no rivers and no bright or dark patches, and from close up the fill should still be visible as a fill. Those two demands pull against each other, and every preset here resolves the tension differently. The most successful are the ones whose variation is large in scale and small in amplitude — a slow drift the eye reads as a tint rather than as an image. The least successful are not the ugliest; they are the ones that look best on a single word, because that is the specimen everyone reaches for and the one that flatters the widest range of mistakes.
None of this can be settled by an assertion. A shader that resolves to a non-null value has proved that it resolves, and nothing more; the question of what it does to four hundred lines of eight-point type is a question about a picture, and pictures are settled by looking at them and by keeping the one that was looked at, so that the next change to any of it has something to be different from. That is what this sheet is: eight pulls, one forme, one ink apiece, held at a moment where every moving field has drifted far enough from its start to show what it will do and not so far that it has left the interesting part behind.
