Here begins the book of the ember gate, set down in the year of the long tide by the wardens of the flooded causeway. Let every reader carry the coal with a steady hand, for the water remembers what the fire forgets, and the reeds keep time against the slow current where the ferry rope hums. The old paths bend around the salt gardens and meet again beneath the bell, where the keepers pour the last light into copper bowls and wait for the morning wind to carry the ash home. Twelve nights the vine was fed, twelve nights the lanterns answered, and on the last the gate stood open wide enough for one small boat, one warden, and the weight of everything they chose to leave behind on the far shore of the salt gardens. Of the twelve, four were kept by the ferry and four by the bell, and the last four were carried down to the water line and set in the reeds, where the tide could read them. It is written that the wardens did not speak on the crossing, and that the rope was warm under their hands from the hands before them. They counted the pilings twice, once going out and once coming back, and both counts agreed, which the older warden took for a good omen and the younger for nothing at all. Beyond the third piling the water runs black even at noon, and the keepers have never agreed whether that is the depth of it or the weed. On the far bank the reeds stand higher than a man and the path through them is kept open by walking it, which is the only upkeep the causeway has ever had. In the third year a warden of the bell set down the order of the crossing, and it is copied here as she wrote it: the coal first, then the rope, then the count, then the name of whoever is going over, spoken once and not written. She notes that the order matters less than the keeping of it, and that a crossing made out of order has never yet drowned anyone, which she offers as an argument for the order rather than against it. Below the bell the water is shallow enough to stand in and the reeds come up through the stones of the old road, and it is there that the lanterns are lit, out of the wind, before they are carried down. Twice in living memory the tide has come over the causeway at noon, and both times the wardens were on the far bank and both times they waited, which is the whole of what the office asks of anyone. The bell itself was cast inland and carried out on the causeway in pieces, which is why it hangs a little out of true and why the note it gives is the note the wardens listen for and no other bell makes. Whoever comes after, let them find the margin kept, the coal banked, and the gate answering to a steady hand alone.
