In the second season the wardens counted the lanterns twice, once for the living channel and once for the drowned road beneath it. The gate takes no coin but memory, says the rubric, and gives back the shape of every hand that held the rope. Draw the circle wide, feed the vine its silver hour, and the causeway will hold one crossing more. So it is written at the water line, and so the tide reads it back to us each night. Keep the margin wide for the vine, keep the corner free for the fox, and let no page close on a coal still warm; the book is a causeway too, and every reader crosses it holding somebody's light. What the second season took, the third gave back doubled, and the keepers wrote it down twice for fear of the tide. The first copy went into the chest under the bell and the second was carried inland, which is why the inland copy is the one that survives and why it is the one that is wrong about the lanterns. Read slowly, and keep the lantern low; the ink is young and the water is patient, and a page turned in haste is a crossing missed. The inland copy gives the lantern count as forty and the drowned copy as thirty-six, and the difference is the four the bell keeps, which the inland scribe had never seen and could not have known to add. That is the whole of the error, and it has stood for three seasons because nobody with both copies has ever been in the same room as anybody who could read them. The wardens themselves count by hand each night and write nothing down, which is why the count in the chest is a copy of a copy and the count in the reeds is the tide's. Let the reader take the larger number and light the four anyway; a lantern lit for nothing is a lantern, and the causeway has never once complained of too much light. As for the fox in the corner: no warden has ever seen it and every warden leaves the corner free, which is either a courtesy to an animal or a courtesy to the wardens who came before, and the wardens do not distinguish. The margin is kept for the vine on the same terms. What a page gives up at its edges it keeps at its centre, says the rubric, and the causeway is a page in that respect as in most others. Here ends the second verse of the ember gate, and the water keeps the rest, as it keeps the road, the rope and the names of every warden who ever walked out over it in the dark.
